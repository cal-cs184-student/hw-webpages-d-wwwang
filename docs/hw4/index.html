Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

Effects of changing the spring constant ks

With a very low ks (e.g. 5 N/m), the cloth is more loose and droopy. During the beginning of the simulation, it droops heavily between the two pinned corners and ripples a lot before settling. The final resting shape has a more defined curvature as a result. With a high ks (e.g. 50,000 N/m), the cloth is more stiff. It hardly droops and oscillates very little before reaching rest. The final resting shape is nearly flat between the two pinned corners.

Effects of changing density

With a very low density (e.g. 1 g/cm²), the cloth is light so gravity has less pull on it relative to the spring forces. It settles quickly and holds a shape close to its rest configuration. With a high density (e.g. 1000 g/cm²), the cloth is much heavier and gravity takes over. It droops significantly more and takes longer to settle. This is similar to lowering ks, but the cause is the mass increasing rather than the springs weakening by changing the spring constant.

With very low damping (e.g. 0%), the cloth swings back and forth like a pendulum and keeps oscillating for a long time before eventually coming to rest. With high damping (e.g. 1%), the cloth moves very slowly and smoothly, reaching its resting state without any noticeable oscillation.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

For sphere collisions, I check whether a point mass's current position is inside the sphere (distance from the origin less than the radius). If it is, I extend the ray from the sphere's origin through the point mass's position out to the surface to get the tangent point. The correction vector goes from last_position to the tangent point, and the new position is last_position plus that correction scaled by (1 - friction).

For plane collisions, I check whether the point mass crossed the plane between its last and current positions by comparing the sign of the dot product of each position (offset by a point on the plane) with the plane normal. If the signs differ, the point mass crossed over. I find the exact intersection point along the path, offset it slightly above the plane by SURFACE_OFFSET on the original side, and apply the same friction-scaled correction from last_position.

With ks = 500 N/m, the cloth is looser and wraps more tightly around the sphere, with more folds draping down the sides. With ks = 5000 N/m (default), it drapes naturally with a smooth curve over the top. With ks = 50000 N/m, the cloth is much stiffer and barely wraps around the sphere. It holds its shape with more rigity and there are fewer folds.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

To avoid the O(n²) cost of checking every pair of point masses, I use spatial hashing. hash_position maps a 3D position into a box of dimensions w x h x t (where w = 3·width/num_width_points, h = 3·height/num_height_points, t = max(w, h)) by flooring each coordinate to its box index and combining them into a single value.

build_spatial_map iterates over all point masses, computes their hash key, and inserts them into the corresponding bucket in the map.

self_collide looks up the bucket for a given point mass and checks each candidate in that bucket. If two point masses are closer than 2x thickness, it computes a correction vector to push them apart to exactly that distance. The final correction applied to the point mass is the average of all pairwise corrections, scaled down by simulation_steps to keep the adjustments smooth.

With low density, the cloth is lighter and the self-collision repulsion forces are strong relative to gravity, so the cloth folds more loosely and spreads out more on the ground. With high density, gravity dominates and the cloth falls faster and compresses more tightly on itself, creating denser, more compact folds.

With a low ks, the cloth is more flexible and crumples into many small, tight folds when it hits itself. With a high ks, the cloth is stiffer and resists bending, so it forms fewer, larger folds and stays more spread out as it settles.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a small program that runs directly on the GPU in parallel across every vertex or fragment at once. There are two main types we use here. The vertex shader runs once per vertex and transforms positions and normals from model space into world space and eventually screen space, writing intermediate values (like world-space position and normal) into outputs for the next stage. The fragment shader then runs once per rasterized fragment (around a pixel) and receives those interpolated values. It uses them to compute the final color, which it writes to out_color. Together they implement the full lighting and material pipeline, where the vertex shader sets up geometry, and the fragment shader decides how each point looks under lighting.

Blinn-Phong splits lighting into three terms. The ambient term is a constant baseline color so nothing is ever completely black. The diffuse term depends on the angle between the surface normal and the light direction. Surfaces facing the light are bright, while surfaces facing away are dark. The specular term adds a shiny highlight by measuring how close the halfway vector (between the light and the viewer) is to the surface normal, raised to a power that controls how sharp the highlight is.

Bump mapping transforms the surface normal at each fragment based on a height map so the lighting interacts as if there is surface detail, but the actual geometry does not change. Displacement mapping goes further, physically moving each vertex along its normal by an amount from the height map. This makes it so the silhouette and depth of the object are actually transformed.

With a coarse sphere mesh (-o 16 -a 16), bump mapping still looks fairly smooth because the normals are computed for each fragment. Displacement mapping on the coarse mesh looks blocky because there are too few vertices to represent the fine height variation. With a dense mesh (-o 128 -a 128), both methods look much more similar, but displacement still has visibly raised geometry at the silhouette.
